# Jingeehas construct framework V2

> **DESIGN PHASE ONLY**
> **NOT YET PSYCHOMETRICALLY VALIDATED**
> **NOT A CLINICAL OR PSYCHOLOGICAL DIAGNOSIS**
> **NOT READY FOR PRODUCTION PSYCHOMETRIC CLAIMS**

## Purpose and boundaries

V2 is an original Mongolian self-report profile of barriers and strengths relevant to weight-loss attempts and eating-related behavior. It is intended to measure dimensional tendencies, not diagnose a mental disorder, explain all causes of weight change, prescribe treatment, or predict kilograms lost.

The core instrument uses a common recall period: **“Сүүлийн 14 хоногт…”** (“During the last 14 days…”). This period is short enough to reduce recall burden while covering weekdays, weekends, and repeated situations. Trait-like habit items retain the same period but ask about automaticity during behaviors that occurred in it.

Unless later cognitive testing supports a better format, core items use:

0. Огт байгаагүй
1. Ховор
2. Заримдаа
3. Олон удаа
4. Бараг үргэлж

Self-efficacy items use an aligned 0–4 confidence scale: **Огт итгэлгүй / Бага / Дунд зэрэг / Их / Маш их итгэлтэй**. Scale-specific differences must be preserved in metadata and not silently treated as identical wording.

## Core constructs

### 1. Emotional eating / affect regulation

- **Definition:** The tendency for emotional states to prompt eating or make eating harder to regulate, including when physical hunger is absent.
- **Included:** stress, sadness, loneliness, frustration, boredom, and eating for short-term soothing.
- **Excluded:** diagnosed mood disorders, ordinary appetite, culturally normative celebration eating, and a single isolated episode.
- **Neighbors:** external cue reactivity, loss of control, self-efficacy, and restrictive rebound.
- **Why it matters:** Eating used to alter affect may compete with planned behavior and can recur under stress.
- **Target items:** 6 candidates; retain 5–6 if factor and content evidence support them.
- **Response scale:** common 0–4 frequency scale.
- **Reverse-coded needs:** 1 carefully worded item about tolerating emotion without turning to food.
- **Expected factor:** one affect-triggered eating factor; evaluate whether boredom separates.
- **Inappropriate interpretations:** depression, anxiety, binge-eating disorder, addiction, or “poor emotional control.”
- **Report-safe interpretation:** “Your responses suggest that eating may become more likely in some emotionally demanding situations.”

### 2. External cue reactivity / food-related cognitive preoccupation

- **Definition:** Attention, desire, or eating prompted by food-related sights, smells, availability, digital cues, or recurring thoughts rather than current physical hunger.
- **Included:** visual/olfactory cues, easy access, delivery-app prompts, difficulty disengaging from food thoughts.
- **Excluded:** physiological hunger, medication effects, food insecurity, planned interest in cooking, and clinical obsession.
- **Neighbors:** habit automaticity, hunger/satiety awareness, uncontrolled eating.
- **Why it matters:** Frequent cue exposure can increase decision load and disrupt intentions.
- **Target items:** 6.
- **Response scale:** common 0–4 frequency scale.
- **Reverse-coded needs:** 1 item on noticing cues without continued preoccupation.
- **Expected factor:** test one factor versus correlated cue-reactivity and preoccupation facets.
- **Inappropriate interpretations:** addiction, obsessive-compulsive disorder, or “food noise disorder.”
- **Report-safe interpretation:** “Food-related cues or thoughts may draw your attention more strongly in daily life.”

### 3. Uncontrolled eating / loss of control

- **Definition:** A subjective sense that starting or continuing to eat is harder to regulate than intended.
- **Included:** difficulty stopping, eating more than intended, feeling carried along once eating begins.
- **Excluded:** objective binge size, diagnostic frequency, compensatory behavior, overeating by social convention, or hunger after deprivation.
- **Neighbors:** restraint, external cues, emotional eating, satiety awareness.
- **Why it matters:** Perceived loss of control can disrupt plans and is more informative than portion size alone.
- **Target items:** 6.
- **Response scale:** common 0–4 frequency scale.
- **Reverse-coded needs:** 1 item about being able to pause after starting.
- **Expected factor:** one subjective-control factor; assess local dependence among stopping items.
- **Inappropriate interpretations:** binge-eating disorder, bulimia nervosa, or a clinical episode count.
- **Report-safe interpretation:** “There were situations where stopping or keeping to the amount you intended felt difficult.”

### 4. Restrictive restraint / all-or-nothing rebound

- **Definition:** Rigid eating rules and dichotomous evaluation of deviations that can lead to abandoning a plan.
- **Included:** inflexible prohibitions, “perfect or failed” thinking, compensatory tightening of non-dangerous rules, rebound after a deviation.
- **Excluded:** flexible planning, medically prescribed restrictions, allergies, religious fasting, and dangerous compensatory behaviors (safety module).
- **Neighbors:** uncontrolled eating, body-image avoidance, self-efficacy, maintenance friction.
- **Why it matters:** Rigid rules may be difficult to maintain and can amplify lapses.
- **Target items:** 6.
- **Response scale:** common 0–4 frequency scale.
- **Reverse-coded needs:** 1–2 items on flexible return after a deviation.
- **Expected factor:** test one factor versus rigidity and rebound facets.
- **Inappropriate interpretations:** eating-disorder diagnosis, “lack of discipline,” or proof that all dietary restraint is harmful.
- **Report-safe interpretation:** “Your plan may become harder to continue when rules leave little room for ordinary variation.”

### 5. Hunger and satiety awareness

- **Definition:** Awareness and usable interpretation of internal hunger and fullness signals during ordinary eating.
- **Included:** detecting early hunger, distinguishing hunger from other urges, noticing comfortable fullness, using those signals when choosing when to start or stop.
- **Excluded:** endocrine or gastrointestinal diagnosis, appetite suppression from medication, food scarcity, and deliberate restriction.
- **Neighbors:** cue reactivity, uncontrolled eating, emotional eating.
- **Why it matters:** Accessible internal signals may help guide timing and amount, while also functioning as a strength.
- **Target items:** 6.
- **Response scale:** common 0–4 frequency scale, scored so higher values mean **greater difficulty** for barrier-profile consistency.
- **Reverse-coded needs:** 3 positively worded awareness items, reverse-coded.
- **Expected factor:** one difficulty factor; test hunger-awareness and satiety-awareness two-factor alternative.
- **Inappropriate interpretations:** interoceptive disorder, metabolic dysfunction, or proof of physiological abnormality.
- **Report-safe interpretation:** “Internal hunger or fullness signals may be less clear or harder to use in some situations.”

### 6. Eating self-efficacy across situations

- **Definition:** Confidence in carrying out a personally chosen, flexible eating intention across common situations.
- **Included:** stress, social settings, time pressure, tempting availability, and recovery after a lapse.
- **Excluded:** moral worth, willpower as a fixed trait, nutrition knowledge, weight-loss expectancy, and ability to follow unsafe restriction.
- **Neighbors:** emotional eating, cue reactivity, restraint, implementation friction.
- **Why it matters:** Situation-specific confidence can support starting, adapting, and resuming behavior.
- **Target items:** 6.
- **Response scale:** 0–4 confidence scale; reverse transform so higher barrier score means lower self-efficacy, while also retaining a strength-direction score.
- **Reverse-coded needs:** none if all items use the same confidence direction; transformation is at scale level, not item wording.
- **Expected factor:** one general factor with possible situation residuals.
- **Inappropriate interpretations:** personality weakness, guaranteed adherence, or treatment readiness.
- **Report-safe interpretation:** “Confidence appears lower in certain situations; this points to where a smaller or more flexible plan may help.”

### 7. Habit automaticity

- **Definition:** The extent to which eating-related actions are initiated with little conscious deliberation in recurring contexts.
- **Included:** automatic reaching, sequence completion, context-triggered action, noticing only after beginning.
- **Excluded:** behavioral frequency alone, identity, cravings, compulsion, and substance-use concepts.
- **Neighbors:** external cues, implementation friction, uncontrolled eating.
- **Why it matters:** Automatic sequences can persist even when intentions change, but can also be redesigned around stable cues.
- **Target items:** 6.
- **Response scale:** common 0–4 frequency scale.
- **Reverse-coded needs:** 1 item about deliberate choice in a recurring situation.
- **Expected factor:** one automaticity factor.
- **Inappropriate interpretations:** addiction, unconscious pathology, or inability to change.
- **Report-safe interpretation:** “Some eating-related actions may begin automatically in familiar situations.”

### 8. Body-image avoidance

- **Definition:** Avoidance of ordinary activities, images, clothing, or social situations because of discomfort about body appearance.
- **Included:** avoiding photographs, mirrors, clothing situations, movement/social participation, and attention to appearance.
- **Excluded:** body dysmorphic disorder, eating-disorder diagnosis, general social anxiety, privacy preference, or physical pain.
- **Neighbors:** restrictive rebound, affect regulation, self-efficacy.
- **Why it matters:** Avoidance can reduce supportive activity, feedback, and engagement and may intensify rigid attempts.
- **Target items:** 6.
- **Response scale:** common 0–4 frequency scale.
- **Reverse-coded needs:** 1 item on participating despite body discomfort.
- **Expected factor:** one avoidance factor; test appearance-checking separately if added later.
- **Inappropriate interpretations:** body dysmorphic disorder, severity of body dissatisfaction, or a judgment about appearance.
- **Report-safe interpretation:** “Body-related discomfort may be narrowing some everyday choices or participation.”

### 9. Implementation and maintenance friction

- **Definition:** Difficulty translating an intended behavior into repeatable actions and maintaining or resuming it when circumstances vary.
- **Included:** unclear next action, excessive setup, no minimum version, disruption by schedule changes, and slow return after interruption.
- **Excluded:** poverty, cost, injury, caregiving load, sleep loss, or lack of social support as psychological traits; these belong in context modules.
- **Neighbors:** self-efficacy, automaticity, restrictive rebound.
- **Why it matters:** Even a sound intention may fail when action steps are not sufficiently specific, small, cued, or recoverable.
- **Target items:** 6.
- **Response scale:** common 0–4 frequency scale.
- **Reverse-coded needs:** 2 items on having a small fallback and resuming after interruption.
- **Expected factor:** test one factor versus initiation and maintenance/resumption facets.
- **Inappropriate interpretations:** executive-function disorder, laziness, noncompliance, or treatment failure.
- **Report-safe interpretation:** “The main difficulty may be making the plan easy to start, repeat, or resume under changing conditions.”

## Separate context modules

Context facts never enter core psychological totals, never increase or decrease core subscale scores, and never create a psychological label. They may qualify interpretation, confidence, safety routing, or starting direction.

| Module | Purpose | Example fields | Output rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sleep/fatigue | Record current sleep opportunity and fatigue burden | duration band, restfulness, daytime fatigue | Separate context card; no causal claim |
| Movement | Describe access and current movement context | mobility, typical movement, preferred forms | Separate context card |
| Injury | Prevent unsuitable movement suggestions | pain, injury, movement limits, clinician restrictions | Guidance boundary; never infer motivation |
| Schedule | Record time variability and caregiving/work constraints | shift work, meal opportunity, predictable windows | Contextual implementation constraint |
| Cost | Record affordability/resource constraints | food, transport, program, equipment affordability | Context fact, not “low commitment” |
| Social support | Record support, conflict, shared-food conditions | practical support, household influence | Context fact; optionally moderator hypothesis in research only |
| Medication | Record appetite/weight-relevant medication context without naming a diagnosis | clinician-supervised use, recent change, side effects | Medical follow-up guidance only |
| Reproductive context | Record pregnancy, postpartum, lactation, cycle, menopause relevance | user-declared state and concern | Separate, sensitive, optional, professionally bounded |
| Urgent safety | Route acute physical danger, active self-harm thoughts, or dangerous compensatory behavior | minimal necessary items | Immediate safety protocol; excluded from scoring and research profiling unless separately consented |

## Cross-construct rules

- Each scored construct must have at least three valid retained items after missingness rules; the operational target is five or more.
- A context answer cannot be used as a proxy item for a psychological construct.
- No score is a diagnosis, cause, treatment match, or clinical cut-off.
- Correlated subscales do not establish an interaction.
- Strength language must be based on a reliable score in the favorable direction, not merely the absence of a barrier.
- Item wording and score direction must remain versioned; reverse coding is never inferred from display text.

## Open scientific decisions

- Whether cue reactivity and cognitive preoccupation form one factor in Mongolian adults.
- Whether hunger and satiety awareness require two correlated factors.
- Whether restrictive rigidity and rebound separate after item reduction.
- Whether implementation initiation and maintenance/resumption separate.
- Whether one common 14-day recall period produces adequate test–retest stability for automaticity.
- Which contextual groups have sufficient sample sizes and ethical justification for invariance testing and norms.
